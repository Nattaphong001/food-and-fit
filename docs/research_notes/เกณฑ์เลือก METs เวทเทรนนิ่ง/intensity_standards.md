## Q1. Garber et al. 2011 ACSM Position Stand: %1RM intensity classification for resistance exercise

### Takeaway
Verified. Garber et al. 2011 **Table 5** classifies resistance exercise relative intensity (%1RM) as **very light <30%, light 30–49%, moderate 50–69%, vigorous 70–84%, near-maximal to maximal ≥85%**. The same table gives RPE (6–20 Borg) only in the cardiorespiratory columns. It gives **no RPE equivalents for resistance exercise**. The body text of the same paper uses a different vocabulary ("moderate to hard", "hard to very hard") that does not line up exactly with Table 5.

### Inferences
- Table 5 is the only source found that uses the words "moderate/vigorous" for resistance-training %1RM. Under it, **vigorous = 70–84% 1RM and near-maximal = ≥85% 1RM**. A threshold such as "≥70% 1RM = vigorous" can be traced verbatim to Garber 2011 Table 5.
- There is an internal vocabulary mismatch. The text calls 60–70% "moderate to hard" and ≥80% "hard to very hard", while Table 5 puts 60–69% in "moderate" and 70–84% in "vigorous". An app should cite **Table 5** for category names, not the text.
- Table 5 classifies **load relative to 1RM**, i.e. weight / 1RM. It does not classify proximity to failure. The app's current "Effort Ratio" = [weight × (1 + reps/30)] / best 1RM is a different quantity: an estimated-1RM ratio, i.e. a proxy for proximity to failure. Table 5's thresholds (70/85%) therefore cannot be applied to Effort Ratio without an interpretive step. Applied to the plain ratio weight / best 1RM, they can be used directly.
- Garber reference 13 (the 2009 position stand) is cited for the ≥80% advanced loading, and "body weight" is explicitly accepted as a resistance modality, but no separate intensity category is given for bodyweight.

### Gaps
- Exact printed page of Table 5 (1337 vs 1341) should be confirmed from the printed journal copy. Text extraction put it after the p. 1340 footer.
- No RPE or OMNI anchor for resistance intensity categories appears in Garber 2011.

## Q2. ACSM Guidelines for Exercise Testing and Prescription (10th/11th/12th ed.) and the ACSM 2009 Progression Models position stand

### Takeaway
Verified: the 2009 ACSM position stand gives goal-based RM zones and %1RM ranges, with rest periods: strength 1–12 RM with emphasis on 1–6 RM and 3–5 min rest; hypertrophy 6–12 RM zone with 1–2 min rest; local muscular endurance 40–60% 1RM, >15 reps, <90 s rest. It does **not** label these zones "moderate" or "vigorous". I could not open GETP 10th/11th/12th ed. text; its classification table is **unverified** here.

- Under the 2009 position stand, the 1–6 RM "heavy loading" zone (≈≥85% 1RM by the NSCA chart below) with 3–5 min rest describes powerlifting-type training. - The 8–12 RM novice zone (60–70% 1RM) overlaps the Compendium 02054 descriptor "8–15 reps at varied resistance".

## Q3. NSCA Essentials of Strength Training and Conditioning: %1RM ↔ repetitions table and goal-based assignments

### Takeaway
Verified (primary, NSCA-published chart): the NSCA Training Load Chart gives **1 RM=100%, 2=95%, 3=93%, 4=90%, 5=87%, 6=85%, 7=83%, 8=80%, 9=77%, 10=75%, 12=70%**. The chart as published stops at 12 RM = 70%. The values "67% = 12" and "65% = 15" in the brief were **not** found in this chart. The goal assignments (strength ≥85% / ≤6 reps; hypertrophy 67–85% / 6–12; endurance ≤67% / ≥12) were found only in secondary sources.

### Inferences
- NSCA chart: **7 RM = 83% 1RM and 6 RM = 85%**. The app's "first-time, avg reps ≤7 → vigorous" rule could be traced like this: 7 RM ≈ 83% 1RM, which Garber Table 5 places in "vigorous (70–84%)". That traceability holds **only if the set is taken to (near) failure**, since RM means the maximum possible reps. A 7-rep set with light weight is not 83% 1RM. By the same chart, 12 RM ≈ 70% 1RM, the lower bound of Garber "vigorous". So any set at a true 12 RM or heavier load would also be "vigorous" by Garber's %1RM criterion.
- Epley (weight × (1 + reps/30)) gives similar values to the chart: for 10 reps, 1/(1+10/30) = 75%, which matches NSCA 10 RM = 75%. For 6 reps, 1/1.2 = 83.3%, versus the chart's 85%. This supports using load/estimated-1RM as a %1RM proxy. The Epley formula's own primary source (Epley B, 1985, *Poundage Chart*, Boyd Epley Workout) was not verified here.

### Gaps
- NSCA 4th ed. goal-assignment and rest-period tables need to be checked in the physical book (Chapter 17) for exact wording, table numbers and page numbers.
- The "67% = 12 reps, 65% = 15 reps" values in the brief were not found in the NSCA chart, which ends at 12 RM = 70%. They may come from the textbook table. **Unverified.**

## Q4. Is "relative intensity" = load / (estimated) 1RM an established definition? What defines heavy/vigorous?

### Takeaway
Yes. Garber 2011 Table 5 labels the %1RM column "Resistance Exercise – Relative Intensity – % 1RM". PAG 2018 defines muscle-strengthening intensity as "how much weight or force is used relative to how much a person is able to lift". The 2009 ACSM position stand defines 1RM-based and RM-based loading schemes. No source was found that defines intensity as *estimated-1RM-of-the-set ÷ best 1RM*, which is the app's current "Effort Ratio". The closest published concept is **proximity to failure / Repetitions in Reserve (RIR)**.

### Cited Findings
- Garber 2011 Table 5 header: "Resistance Exercise / Relative Intensity / % 1RM". — [Garber 2011 PDF](https://www.abom.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/12/Quantity_and_Quality_of_Exercise_for_Developing.26-002.pdf) — **Verified (primary)**
- PAG 2018 (verbatim): "Muscle-strengthening activity has three components: Intensity, or how much weight or force is used relative to how much a person is able to lift; Frequency ...; and Sets and repetitions ...". Glossary (verbatim): "For muscle-strengthening activities, intensity frequently is expressed as the amount of weight lifted or moved." — [PAG 2nd ed. PDF](https://odphp.health.gov/sites/default/files/2019-09/Physical_Activity_Guidelines_2nd_edition.pdf) — **Verified (primary)**
- ACSM 2009 (verbatim): "proper loading during RT encompasses one or more of the following loading schemes: 1) increasing load based on a percentage of 1 RM, 2) increasing absolute load based on a targeted repetition number, or 3) increasing loading within a prescribed zone (e.g., 8–12 RM)." — [ACSM 2009 PDF](https://tourniquets.org/wp-content/uploads/PDFs/ACSM-Progression-models-in-resistance-training-for-healthy-adults-2009.pdf) — **Verified (primary)**
- **RIR-based RPE (Zourdos 2016; Helms 2016):**
  - Citation: Zourdos MC, Klemp A, Dolan C, Quiles JM, Schau KA, Jo E, Helms E, Esgro B, Duncan S, Garcia Merino S, Blanco R. Novel resistance training–specific rating of perceived exertion scale measuring repetitions in reserve. *J Strength Cond Res.* 2016;30(1):267–275. doi:10.1519/JSC.0000000000001049 — [PubMed 26049792](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26049792/). Author list beyond the first author is **unverified**.
  - Helms ER, Cronin J, Storey A, Zourdos MC. Application of the repetitions in reserve-based rating of perceived exertion scale for resistance training. *Strength Cond J.* 2016;38(4):42–49. doi:10.1519/SSC.0000000000000218 — [PMC4961270](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4961270/)
  - Verbatim from Helms: "the scale has subjective descriptors of effort for values below 5 (1–2 RPE = 'little to no effort,' 3–4 RPE = 'light effort')".
  - The core mapping RPE 10 = 0 RIR (maximum effort), 9 = 1 RIR, 8 = 2 RIR, 7 = 3 RIR, 5–6 = 4–6 RIR is widely reproduced from Zourdos Table 1. The table is an image in PMC and I could not text-verify it: **treat the exact descriptors as unverified**.
  - Helms (verbatim): "we advise primarily (but not exclusively) using repetitions in the range of 6–12, with an RIR-based RPE of 8–10 (RIR 0–2) depending on phase of training." Force-dominant power: "relatively heavy intensities (>80% 1RM) ... low repetition sets (1–5) stopping sufficiently short of volitional failure (RIR 2–3)".
  - Helms Table 2, the %1RM–reps–RPE conversion, is "based on the mean RIR-based RPE scores reported by the experienced squatters ... Values for percentage 1RM repetition combinations besides single repetitions at 90 and 100% 1RM and 8 repetitions at 70% 1RM are estimations ... this chart is based on the barbell back squat, and this relationship may change with machine-based, single-joint, or upper-body exercises." Also: "it would not be appropriate to use this scale for low to moderate intensity, high-velocity power training (under 80% 1RM) if the goal is to have an accurate gauge of RIR."

  — [Helms 2016, PMC4961270](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4961270/) — **Verified (primary)** except the RPE 5–10 descriptor table noted above.

### Inferences
- The most defensible published "heavy/vigorous" thresholds on a %1RM basis are:
  - Garber 2011 Table 5: vigorous ≥70% 1RM; near-maximal ≥85%.
  - ACSM 2009: heavy loading = 1–6 RM (80–100% for advanced strength; 85–100% "heavy loading" for power).
  - NSCA: strength ≥85% / ≤6 reps, secondary-verified only.
- The app's Effort Ratio ≥ 0.90 threshold is not in any of these. It amounts to a proximity-to-failure criterion (≈ RIR 4–5 by Epley back-calculation), and the only published proximity-to-failure scale (Zourdos/Helms) does not use the words moderate/vigorous and does not map to METs. A committee-traceable alternative is to compute **plain %1RM = weight ÷ best estimated 1RM** and apply the Garber Table 5 cut-points (≥70% vigorous), optionally cross-checked with the NSCA chart (≤12 RM ≈ ≥70%).

### Gaps
- No published source defines vigorous resistance training in terms of an "estimated-1RM ratio" or session-averaged effort.

## Q5. Sources mapping resistance-training intensity to METs or to moderate/vigorous physical activity

### Takeaway
No authoritative source maps %1RM categories to MET values. WHO 2020 and PAG 2018 require muscle-strengthening activity at "moderate or greater intensity" but define moderate/vigorous only in METs (3.0–5.9 / ≥6.0) or on a relative 0–10 effort scale (5–6 / 7–8), not in %1RM. The 2024 Adult Compendium gives the METs per activity descriptor, and its resistance descriptors use effort/reps words, not %1RM.

### Cited Findings
- **WHO 2020:** Bull FC, Al-Ansari SS, Biddle S, et al. ## Q6. How the sources treat bodyweight exercises

### Takeaway
All sources accept bodyweight as a legitimate resistance modality. None gives a %1RM-based intensity scale for bodyweight exercises. The Compendium is the only source with bodyweight-specific METs: 3.0 general and 6.5 high intensity (02057).

### Cited Findings
- Garber 2011 Table 2 (verbatim): "A variety of exercise equipment and/or body weight can be used to perform these exercises. [A]" — [Garber 2011 PDF](https://www.abom.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/12/Quantity_and_Quality_of_Exercise_for_Developing.26-002.pdf) — **Verified (primary)**
- PAG 2018 (verbatim): "Muscle-strengthening activity can also be done by using elastic bands or body weight for resistance (climbing a tree or doing push-ups, for example)". Also "doing calisthenics that use body weight for resistance (such as push-ups, pull-ups, and planks)". Youth: "Muscle-strengthening activities count if they involve a moderate or greater level of effort". — [PAG 2nd ed. PDF](https://odphp.health.gov/sites/default/files/2019-09/Physical_Activity_Guidelines_2nd_edition.pdf) — **Verified (primary)**
- Compendium 2024: 02056 = 3.0 "general" and 02057 = 6.5 "high intensity" bodyweight resistance exercises. — local extracted Compendium text (see Q5)

### Inferences
- Classifying all bodyweight work as 02056 (3.0) is consistent with the Compendium "general" row. Note, however, that the Compendium itself offers a high-intensity bodyweight row (02057, 6.5). A rule that never uses 02057 is a design choice, not a limitation imposed by the Compendium.
- PAG's criterion "to the point at which it would be difficult to do another repetition" (≈ proximity to failure) is the only published, modality-neutral effort criterion that could apply to bodyweight exercises. It is not numerically defined.

### Gaps
- No source found gives numeric criteria (reps, tempo, rest) separating "general" from "high intensity" bodyweight resistance in the Compendium.
