# Effort / proximity-to-failure and e1RM literature — does "Effort Ratio" (set e1RM ÷ best e1RM) have a published counterpart and a defensible threshold?

Research date: 2026-09-27. Primary full texts read directly: Reynolds et al. 2006 (PDF), Halperin et al. 2022 (accepted-manuscript PDF), Nuzzo et al. 2024 (PMC full text + Springer PDF), Bishop et al. 2025 ACSM–ESSA statement (MSSE PDF), Garber et al. 2011 ACSM position stand (PDF). Abstracts verified via Europe PMC: Zourdos 2016, Helms 2016, Steele 2017, Greig 2020, Bastos 2024. Citation metadata verified via Crossref: LeSuer 1997, Brzycki 1993. Anything else is flagged.

## Q1. 1RM prediction equations (Epley, Brzycki, LeSuer 1997, Reynolds 2006, DiStasio 2014): origin, validation, accuracy and rep range

### Takeaway
Epley's formula comes from a coaching book, not a peer-reviewed study: Epley B., "Poundage chart", *Boyd Epley Workout*, 1985, p. 86. It was validated only later, by other authors. Reynolds et al. (2006) found that Epley's equation was among the most accurate linear equations on 5RM data. Every equation got less accurate at 10RM. The authors concluded that "no more than 10 repetitions should be used in linear equations to estimate 1RM". This peer-reviewed statement supports the app's filter of reps 1–10 on Best 1RM.

### Cited Findings
- **Epley primary source (not peer-reviewed).** Reference 11 in Reynolds et al. 2006 reads "EPLEY, B. Poundage chart. In: Boyd Epley Workout. Lincoln, NE: Body Enterprises, 1985. p. 86." It is a book or chart and reports no validation data — [Reynolds et al. 2006, J Strength Cond Res 20(3):584–592, PDF](https://www.unm.edu/~rrobergs/478RMStrengthPrediction.pdf)
- **Reynolds, Gordon & Robergs (2006).** Citation: J Strength Cond Res 2006;20(3):584–592; PMID 16937972 ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16937972/)). Design: 70 subjects (34 men, 36 women) aged 18–69 completed 1, 5, 10 and 20RM on chest press and leg press. Verbatim abstract: "Multiple regression analyses revealed that the 5RM data produced the greatest prediction accuracy, with R2 data for 5, 10, and 20RM conditions being LP: 0.974, 0.933, 0.915; CP: 0.993, 0.976, and 0.955 … Dynamic muscular strength (1RM) can be accurately estimated from multiple repetition testing. Data reveal that no more than 10 repetitions should be used in linear equations to estimate 1RM for the LP and CP actions." — [Reynolds et al. 2006 PDF](https://www.unm.edu/~rrobergs/478RMStrengthPrediction.pdf)
- **Reynolds et al. 2006, Epley vs other equations** (cross-validation group, n = 20). Verbatim: "our equations, and those of Abadie (1) and Epley (11), had the smallest mean residuals, with only a slight trend for overestimation. The equations of Brzycki (7), Lander (18), and O'Connor (26) all underestimated LP strength. All equations were less accurate when the 10RM rather than 5RM data was used. The nonlinear equations of Lombardi (19) and Mayhew (21) were less accurate than all linear equations." The Table 5 footnote gives each author's guideline: "Bryzcki and Epley [≤]10 reps" (the inequality symbol was lost in PDF extraction and is read here as ≤) — [Reynolds et al. 2006 PDF](https://www.unm.edu/~rrobergs/478RMStrengthPrediction.pdf)
- **Reynolds et al. 2006, why 1RM is estimated at all.** Verbatim: "for some populations, age and preexisting medical conditions may be contraindications to the safe completion of 1RM testing." — [Reynolds et al. 2006 PDF](https://www.unm.edu/~rrobergs/478RMStrengthPrediction.pdf)
- **Brzycki (1993)**, verified via Crossref: Brzycki M. "Strength Testing—Predicting a One-Rep Max from Reps-to-Fatigue." J Phys Educ Recreat Dance 1993;64(1):88–90. doi:10.1080/07303084.1993.10606684 — [Crossref DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/07303084.1993.10606684). It appeared in a professional journal (JOPERD), not a research journal.
- **LeSuer et al. (1997)**, verified via Crossref: LeSuer DA, McCormick JH, Mayhew JL, Wasserstein RL, Arnold MD. "The Accuracy of Prediction Equations for Estimating 1-RM Performance in the Bench Press, Squat, and Deadlift." J Strength Cond Res 1997;11(4):211–213. doi:10.1519/00124278-199711000-00001 — [JSCR abstract page](https://journals.lww.com/nsca-jscr/abstract/1997/11000/the_accuracy_of_prediction_equations_for.1.aspx)
- **LeSuer 1997 content — secondary sources only; abstract NOT verified.** The publisher page returned 402 and Semantic Scholar has the abstract elided. Secondary sources say: 67 untrained college students (40 men, 27 women) were tested on 7 equations for bench press, squat and deadlift; correlations were high (r > 0.95); Mayhew and Wathen were the most accurate; all equations significantly underestimated deadlift 1RM; and "RTF ≤10 can accurately predict 1-RM lifts". Source: search-engine summary of [SIU graduate paper "Validation of the Brzycki and Epley Equations…"](https://opensiuc.lib.siu.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1744&context=gs_rp) and the [JSCR abstract page](https://journals.lww.com/nsca-jscr/abstract/1997/11000/the_accuracy_of_prediction_equations_for.1.aspx). Treat these as unverified until the abstract is read.
- **Mechanism for the ≤10 limit (secondary).** Brzycki's formula assumes a linear load–reps relationship, which holds for ≤10 reps and becomes nonlinear above that — [SIU graduate paper (secondary)](https://opensiuc.lib.siu.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1744&context=gs_rp). Reynolds 2006 independently documents a nonlinear decrease in load with reps: "CP: linear Sy.x 2.6 kg, nonlinear Sy.x 0.2 kg" — [Reynolds PDF](https://www.unm.edu/~rrobergs/478RMStrengthPrediction.pdf)
- **Commonly repeated claim — UNVERIFIED, do not cite.** A claim that Reynolds 2006 found prediction error of "±2% at 3 reps, ±5% at 5 reps, ±10% at 10 reps" appeared only in a search-engine summary of calculator websites. It is not in the Reynolds paper, which tested 5, 10 and 20RM, not 3RM.

### Inferences
- For "Epley is accurate when reps ≤ 10", cite Reynolds et al. 2006. It is peer-reviewed, cross-validated, and compares Epley directly. Brzycki 1993 or the Epley book are weaker for this purpose.
- Keeping singles (reps = 1) in the Best 1RM filter is defensible without extra citation. A 1-rep set is by definition a direct 1RM attempt if taken to failure, and the Epley factor at r = 1 is only 1.033. It is still not a true 1RM unless the set was maximal.
- Every estimation paper computes 1RM from sets taken **to failure** (RM tests / reps-to-fatigue). Training-log sets are usually stopped short of failure. Epley then underestimates the true 1RM, and the size of the error depends on RIR. This limitation matters for "Best 1RM from history".

### Gaps
- DiStasio (2014): no source found in this search, so it cannot be verified. Likely the SIU graduate research paper on validating Brzycki/Epley, but that is unconfirmed; do not cite.
- The verbatim LeSuer 1997 abstract was not retrieved (paywalled). Its "≤10 reps" statement is second-hand only.

## Q2. RIR-based RPE (Zourdos 2016; Helms 2016; Helms 2018; Tuchscherer): what effort level counts as "hard"?

### Takeaway
The RIR-based RPE scale maps RPE 10 to 0 RIR, RPE 9 to 1 RIR, and so on. It is validated in peer-reviewed work: Zourdos 2016 (JSCR) and Helms 2016 (SCJ). RIR ratings are most accurate within about 0–3 reps of failure. None of these papers defines "vigorous" for energy expenditure; they concern load prescription.

### Cited Findings
- **Zourdos et al. 2016.** J Strength Cond Res 2016;30(1):267–275. doi:10.1519/JSC.0000000000001049. PMID 26049792. Verbatim abstract: "Subjects reported an RPE value that corresponded to an RIR value (RPE-10 = 0-RIR, RPE-9 = 1-RIR, and so forth)." Also: "In ES, there was a strong inverse relationship between average velocity and RPE at all percentages (r = -0.88 …) … The RIR-based RPE scale is a practical method to regulate daily training load and provide feedback during a 1RM test." — [PubMed 26049792](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26049792/) (abstract text via Europe PMC)
- **Helms, Cronin, Storey & Zourdos 2016.** "Application of the Repetitions in Reserve-Based Rating of Perceived Exertion Scale for Resistance Training." Strength Cond J 2016;38(4):42–49. doi:10.1519/SSC.0000000000000218. PMID 27531969; PMC4961270. The abstract says the scale "adjusts loads automatically to match athlete capabilities on a set-to-set basis and may more accurately gauge intensity at near-limit loads." — [PMC4961270](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4961270/)
- **Helms 2016 caveat** on their RPE↔%1RM chart. Verbatim: "This chart is not without limitations as it is based on the mean scores specific to the trained lifters in this study only". Also "there are significant differences in how many repetitions can be performed at the same percentage of 1RM by different individuals", and "experienced lifters are more consistent at gauging RIR as they approach failure." — [PMC4961270](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4961270/)
- **Helms et al. 2018.** "RPE vs. Percentage 1RM Loading in Periodized Programs Matched for Sets and Repetitions." Front Physiol 2018;9:247. doi:10.3389/fphys.2018.00247. PMID 29628895.
  - Weekly target RPE ranged 5–7 up to 8–10.
  - Load rule, verbatim: "For every 0.5 RPE above or below the upper or lower RPE threshold, respectively, load was decreased or increased by 2%."
  - Verbatim: "Researchers have reported males and females to determine RIR accurately (within ~1 repetition) during the leg and chest press exercises when sets are performed within 0–3 repetitions from failure."
  - Mean %1RM in the RPE group: squat 79.73 ± 4.44%, bench press 84.14 ± 2.02%.
  - Source: [Frontiers full text](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2018.00247/full)

### Inferences
- The RIR scale is well established. Within it, the "near-limit" zone where ratings are reliable is 0–3 RIR. The app's ≈4–5 RIR sits outside the zone that Helms/Zourdos describe as accurate.
- Helms 2018 shows that practitioners use RPE-driven %1RM adjustment. That is conceptually close to the app's per-set ratio, but it is a prescription tool, not a classifier of energy expenditure.

### Gaps
- Tuchscherer RPE chart (Reactive Training Systems): no peer-reviewed primary source was found. It is a coaching resource and should not be cited as science.
- The Helms 2016 Table 1 descriptors (e.g., RPE 5–6 = "4–6 RIR") were not extractable verbatim; the table is an image in PMC.

## Q3. Proximity to failure / "intensity of effort" (Steele 2017; Refalo 2023; Halperin 2022) and published RIR thresholds for "high" effort

### Takeaway
The strongest and most recent citable threshold is the joint ACSM–ESSA statement (Bishop et al., MSSE 2025). It recommends RIR, not %1RM, for resistance exercise intensity. Its Table 2 as extracted from the PDF classifies intensity by RIR:
- **>8 RIR:** Very low
- **7–8 RIR:** Low
- **4–6 RIR:** Moderate
- **2–3 RIR:** High
- **<2 RIR:** Very high

Under this table, the app's "≈4–5 RIR" threshold is **Moderate, not High**. The same statement also replaces the word "vigorous" with "High".

### Cited Findings
- **Bishop DJ, Beck B, Biddle SJH, et al.** "Physical Activity and Exercise Intensity Terminology: A Joint American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) Expert Statement and Exercise and Sport Science Australia (ESSA) Consensus Statement." Med Sci Sports Exerc 2025;57(11):2599–2613. doi:10.1249/MSS.0000000000003795. Also published in J Sci Med Sport. Sources: [MSSE via Ovid](https://www.ovid.com/jnls/acsm-msse/fulltext/10.1249/mss.0000000000003795~physical-activity-and-exercise-intensity-terminology-a-joint); [JSAMS version](https://www.jsams.org/article/S1440-2440(24)00559-0/fulltext); full-text PDF copy read at [fisiologiadelejercicio.com](https://www.fisiologiadelejercicio.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/Physical-Activity-and-Exercise-Intensity-Terminology.pdf)
  - **Table 2**, "Proposed classifications and criteria used to estimate inactivity and physical activity/exercise intensity". The column layout was reconstructed from the PDF text and should be checked against the printed table.

    | Category | Resistance: Reps in Reserve (RIR) | RPE10 | RPE20 |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | Inactive | Inactive | 0 | 6 |
    | Very low | >8 | <2 | 9 |
    | Low | 7–8 | 2–3 | 10–11 |
    | Moderate | 4–6 | 4–5 | 12–14 |
    | High | 2–3 | 6–7 | 15–16 |
    | Very high | <2 | 8–10 | 17 |

  - Verbatim on %1RM: "For resistance training, we do not recommend prescribing exercise intensity based on %1RM (i.e., a measure of load). Instead, we recommend using RIR (an estimation of how many more repetitions can be completed before neuromuscular failure) as a better measure of "how hard" the exercise feels."
  - Verbatim on load vs failure: resistance intensity is "determined not only by load but also by the progression toward neuromuscular failure (sometimes expressed as repetitions in reserve (RIR))". Also: "a load of 50% 1RM may be Low to Moderate intensity over the initial repetitions, but the intensity becomes Very High when the number of repetitions continues to neuromuscular failure." And: "well-trained lifters could perform 6–26 repetitions at 70% of their individual 1RM".
  - Verbatim on terminology: "we propose a standard terminology … comprising five exercise intensities: very low, low, moderate, high, and very high … we suggest not using descriptors such as light, heavy, weak, or strong". The text also says that although "vigorous" is well established, "High" is preferred.
  - Verbatim on METs: "we are not able to recommend the markers that appear in many exercise guidelines (e.g., %VO2max, %HRmax, %HRR, and METs)".
  - Verbatim caveats: RIR "is more reliable with experienced lifters", and "When these two measures [RIR and RPE] do not agree, it is prudent … to select the highest category".
  - Its RIR references: Lovegrove et al. JSCR 2022;36(10):2696–2700; Refalo et al. Sports Med Open 2023;9:10; Zourdos et al. 2016.

The content was not read. The existence of the debate is worth noting in the thesis.

PMID 28044366. Verbatim abstract: "Set endpoints in RT are often considered to be proximity to momentary failure and are thought to be a primary variable determining effort in RT." — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/mus.25557)

### Inferences
- ACSM–ESSA 2025 is a peer-reviewed, official ACSM pronouncement. It gives exactly the missing citation for an RIR-based intensity threshold: **High = 2–3 RIR, Very high = <2 RIR, Moderate = 4–6 RIR.** If the app's "heavy" level should mean ACSM–ESSA "High", the cut-off should be RIR ≤ 3, not ≈4–5.
- ACSM–ESSA also explicitly declines to endorse METs as intensity markers. A thesis that pairs RIR-based classification with Compendium METs should acknowledge that it combines two frameworks, and that the pairing is the author's own design choice.

### Gaps
- The verbatim "intensity of effort" definition in Steele 2017 and in Steele et al. 2022 (J Sport Health Sci, "Intensity of effort and momentary failure…: a binary question for a continuous variable?") was not retrieved; both pages returned 403 — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2095254622000400)
- No paper was found that defines "high effort = 0–3 RIR" in the Steele/Refalo line with a verbatim quote. The 0–3 RIR figure is verifiable only in Helms 2018 (the accuracy window) and ACSM–ESSA Table 2 (High 2–3, Very high <2).

## Q4. Is "relative intensity = load / estimated 1RM" (or set e1RM ÷ best e1RM) used in published research on training logs or autoregulation?

### Takeaway
No peer-reviewed paper was found that defines the app's exact metric: one set's Epley e1RM divided by the athlete's historical best e1RM. Autoregulation reviews (Greig 2020) describe adjusting load to daily performance or perceived capability as a general framework. The established practice is to estimate relative intensity from RIR/RPE (Helms 2016/2018), or to estimate RIR from load and reps using population tables (Nuzzo 2024). A historical-best e1RM ratio is not an established practice.

### Cited Findings
- **Greig L, Stephens Hemingway BH, Aspe RR, Cooper K, Comfort P, Swinton PA.** "Autoregulation in Resistance Training: Addressing the Inconsistencies." Sports Med 2020;50:1873–1887 (page range from memory; not verified here). doi:10.1007/s40279-020-01330-8. PMID 32813181. Verbatim abstract: "Autoregulation is a process that is used to manipulate training based primarily on the measurement of an individual's performance or their perceived capability to perform … A primary limitation of previous research includes inconsistent use of key terminology". The abstract does not mention e1RM ratios — [PubMed 32813181](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32813181/)
- **Helms 2016.** The RIR-RPE scale "adjusts loads automatically to match athlete capabilities on a set-to-set basis". This is the daily-capability idea in its published form, but it relies on the athlete's own RIR rating, not an e1RM ratio — [PMC4961270](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4961270/)
- **Robinson ZP et al. 2024** (Sports Med; doi:10.1007/s40279-024-02069-2). "Exploring the Dose–Response Relationship Between Estimated Resistance Training Proximity to Failure, Strength Gain, and Muscle Hypertrophy: A Series of Meta-Regressions." The authors *estimated* RIR retrospectively from the training descriptions in included studies and cite Nuzzo et al. The exact estimation method was **not verified**: the Springer page redirected to login, and the preprint says only "the RIR associated with each effect in the analysis was estimated based on the available descriptions of the training interventions in each study." — [SportRxiv preprint](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/295)
- **Non-peer-reviewed preprint, not verified.** "A Weight-Dependent 1RM Prediction Equation Optimized on 303,494 Near-Failure Sets Across 388 Exercises" (arXiv 2603.17495, 2026) models e1RM from large training-log data. It is a preprint and should not be cited as established evidence — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.17495)

### Inferences
- The app's metric is algebraically `(load ÷ best e1RM) × (1 + reps/30)`: relative load (%e1RM) multiplied by the Epley rep factor. Under Epley's model, with best e1RM equal to the true current 1RM, the ratio equals `(30+r)/(30+r+RIR)`. It is therefore an Epley-model proxy for RIR, not a new construct. It can be presented as "an estimate of proximity to failure derived from the Epley model", citing Epley via Reynolds 2006 plus ACSM–ESSA for the RIR construct. It cannot be presented as a published metric.
- Two stacked assumptions carry no published validation for this use:
  1. The Epley reps–%1RM curve is assumed to hold for this lifter and exercise. Nuzzo shows large individual variation (Q5).
  2. The historical best e1RM is assumed to equal the current true 1RM. Best e1RM comes from sub-failure sets, so it tends to be an underestimate. That inflates the ratio and understates RIR.

### Gaps
- No published validation was found of "historical best e1RM" as a denominator for daily relative intensity.
- The Greig 2020 page range was not verified.

## Q5. Published %1RM ↔ reps-to-failure tables that could replace inverting Epley

### Takeaway
Nuzzo, Pinto, Nosaka & Steele (Sports Med 2024) is the definitive meta-regression: 269 studies, 7,289 individuals, 952 tests. At a given %1RM, people complete more reps than Epley implies: about 5 reps at 90% 1RM and about 15 at 70% (main model). Epley implies about 3.3 and about 12.9. Between-person SD is large: 2.5 reps at 80% 1RM and 4.4 reps at 60%. Any fixed ratio therefore maps to a wide RIR band.

### Cited Findings
- **Citation.** Nuzzo JL, Pinto MD, Nosaka K, Steele J. "Maximal Number of Repetitions at Percentages of the One Repetition Maximum: A Meta-Regression and Moderator Analysis of Sex, Age, Training Status, and Exercise." Sports Med 2024;54(2):303–321 (online 4 Oct 2023). doi:10.1007/s40279-023-01937-7. PMC10933212 — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10933212/); [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-023-01937-7)
- **Scope.** "952 repetitions-to-failure tests completed by 7,289 individuals in 452 groups from 269 studies". Moderators: "little influences of sex, age, or training status … the general main model REPS ~ %1RM table can be applied to all individuals and to all exercises other than the bench press and leg press." — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-023-01937-7)
- **Point estimates.** Verbatim (PDF): "In Fig. 2, estimates at 90% and 70% 1RM are ~5 and ~15 repetitions, respectively." The textbook table they compare against gives 4 and 11. For bench press at 90/80/70% 1RM: "~4, ~9, and ~14"; for leg press "~9, ~13, and ~19". Bench at 80% and 70%: 8.8 [95% CI 7.7–10.1] and 14.1 [12.4–16.1]. Leg press: 13.1 [9.8–17.5] and 19.0 [14.2–25.5] — [Springer PDF](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s40279-023-01937-7.pdf)
- **Individual variability.** Verbatim: "at 80% 1RM, the estimate for the SD about the point estimate is 2.51 repetitions, whereas at 60% 1RM the estimate is 4.36 repetitions." — [PMC10933212](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10933212/)
- **Method.** Mean repetitions were modelled with "natural cubic splines with knots at 60% and 80% of 1RM". The full table covers 15–95% 1RM in 5% steps but is published as an image (Fig. 2) — [PMC10933212](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10933212/)
- **Unverified secondary values.** A search summary gave approximately 3 reps at 95%, 7 at 85%, 10 at 80% and 12 at 75%. These did not come from the Fig. 2 image and should be checked against the figure before use — [Stronger by Science summary](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/reps-percentage/)
- **Reynolds 2006 %1RM data.** Mean %1RM for 5, 10 and 20RM was leg press 85.91, 70.10 and 51.58%. For chest press, the text layout was garbled and only 61.61% for 20RM is certain — [Reynolds PDF](https://www.unm.edu/~rrobergs/478RMStrengthPrediction.pdf)
- **ACSM–ESSA 2025** cites Cooke et al. 2019 (JSCR 33(3):890–895): well-trained lifters did "6–26 repetitions at 70% of their individual 1RM" — [ACSM–ESSA PDF](https://www.fisiologiadelejercicio.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/Physical-Activity-and-Exercise-Intensity-Terminology.pdf)

### Inferences
- Epley versus Nuzzo, as computed here. Epley implies `reps = 30 × (1/%1RM − 1)`. At 90%: 3.3 reps, against Nuzzo ~5. At 80%: 7.5, against Nuzzo about 9 for bench. At 70%: 12.9, against Nuzzo ~15.
  - Real lifters can do roughly 1.5–2 more reps than Epley assumes at a given load.
  - So when a set's reps are fixed, the student's Epley inversion likely **underestimates the true RIR** by about 1–2 reps.
  - A ratio of 0.90 therefore probably corresponds to about 5–7 RIR in the average person, not 4–5. That is ACSM–ESSA "Moderate".
  - This is a model-based inference, not a published result.
- A defensible replacement for inverting Epley: use the Nuzzo main-model Fig. 2 table to estimate predicted max reps at the set's load as a fraction of best e1RM. Then take estimated RIR = predicted max reps − reps performed, and classify with the ACSM–ESSA RIR bands. This follows the retrospective RIR-estimation logic of Robinson et al. 2024 (method unverified, see Q4). Every step would be cited, though the combined pipeline is still the author's own.

### Gaps
- The exact numbers in Nuzzo Fig. 2 (15–95% in 5% steps) could not be extracted as text because the table is an image. Read them from the figure before use.
- Richens & Cleather 2014 was not located in this search, so it cannot be verified. Likewise, the NSCA/Baechle textbook Table 1 was seen only as described by Nuzzo (their ref. 7).

## Q6. Literature linking resistance-exercise RPE/effort or %1RM to METs or moderate/vigorous physical-activity classes

### Takeaway
The only published scheme that labels resistance exercise "vigorous" is **load-based**. The ACSM 2011 position stand (Garber et al.) classifies resistance exercise by %1RM:
- **<30%:** very light
- **30–49%:** light
- **50–69%:** moderate
- **70–84%:** vigorous
- **≥85%:** near maximal

ACSM–ESSA 2025 now advises against %1RM and uses RIR bands instead (High = 2–3 RIR). No published source maps RIR or %1RM directly to Compendium MET codes 02050 or 02054.

### Cited Findings
- **Garber CE, et al.** "Quantity and Quality of Exercise for Developing and Maintaining Cardiorespiratory, Musculoskeletal, and Neuromotor Fitness in Apparently Healthy Adults." Med Sci Sports Exerc 2011;43(7):1334–1359. doi:10.1249/MSS.0b013e318213fefb. PMID 21694556 (PMID from memory; DOI verified in PDF).
  - Table 5, "Classification of exercise intensity: relative and absolute exercise intensity for cardiorespiratory endurance and resistance exercise". The resistance row, "Relative Intensity % 1RM", gives <30 (very light), 30–49 (light), 50–69 (moderate), 70–84 (vigorous) and ≥85 (near maximal).
  - For cardiorespiratory work, the same table gives absolute MET bands of 3.0–5.9 (moderate) and 6.0–8.7 (vigorous), and RPE 12–13 ("somewhat hard") versus 14–17 ("somewhat hard to very hard").
  - Source: ACSM position stand PDF, extracted locally; the DOI is printed in the paper.
- **ACSM–ESSA 2025, Table 1**, lists current criteria, including "Resistance Exercise … % 1RM (Howley 2001)": <30 / 30–49 / 50–69 / 70–84 / ≥85. It then proposes the RIR scheme instead — [ACSM–ESSA PDF](https://www.fisiologiadelejercicio.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/Physical-Activity-and-Exercise-Intensity-Terminology.pdf)
- **OMNI-RES.** Robertson RJ, Goss FL, Rutkowski J, et al. "Concurrent validation of the OMNI perceived exertion scale for resistance exercise." Med Sci Sports Exerc 2003;35(2):333–341. Citation taken from the ACSM–ESSA reference list, ref. 69. ACSM–ESSA describes OMNI-RES as a "0 to 10 category rating scale that consists of images depicting a weight lifter … (extremely easy to extremely hard)".
- **OMNI-RES and energy cost.** A Journal of Human Kinetics study (PMID 23486188) reported that OMNI-RES RPE correlated with energy cost only at low intensities. The detail "up to 24% of 1-RM" came from a search summary and was not verified — [PMC3588892](https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3588892)

### Inferences
- If the committee wants the "vigorous" label to trace to a published classification, the most direct option is **Garber 2011 / ACSM Table 5: ≥70% 1RM = vigorous**. For the app, that means session mean load ÷ best e1RM ≥ 0.70. Two caveats apply:
  1. It uses e1RM rather than a measured 1RM, so this remains a stated adaptation.
  2. ACSM–ESSA 2025 has since discouraged %1RM as an intensity measure.
- The more modern option is ACSM–ESSA 2025: estimated RIR ≤ 3 = "High". Under the app's own Epley model, RIR ≤ 3 corresponds to an Effort Ratio of at least `(30+r)/(33+r)`. That is 0.921 at r = 5, 0.927 at r = 8, 0.930 at r = 10, and 0.933 at r = 12, so roughly **≥0.93**, not 0.90. For comparison, 0.90 gives RIR = (30+r)/9: 3.9 at r = 5, 4.4 at r = 10, 4.7 at r = 12. These values match the student's own derivation and fall in the ACSM–ESSA "Moderate" band (4–6).
- Mapping a High-effort class to Compendium 02050 (6.0 MET) remains the author's interpretation either way. No study links RIR or %1RM classes to MET values, and ACSM–ESSA rejects METs as intensity markers.

### Gaps
- No study found measures the MET cost of resistance-training sessions stratified by RIR or %1RM that would validate the 3.5 vs 6.0 MET split.
- The Garber 2011 PMID (21694556) was not re-verified in this session.

## Q7. Synthesis — evaluating the app's rule (Effort Ratio ≥ 0.90 → vigorous; no history and reps ≤ 7 → vigorous)

### Takeaway
The concepts behind Effort Ratio are citable: e1RM via Epley ≤10 reps (Reynolds 2006) and proximity to failure/RIR as intensity (Zourdos 2016; ACSM–ESSA 2025). The ratio itself and the 0.90 threshold are not published. Worse, 0.90 falls in the ACSM–ESSA **Moderate** RIR band (4–6), not High (2–3), and likely lands even further from failure once Nuzzo's reps-to-%1RM data are used.

### Cited Findings
- RIR bands: Moderate 4–6, High 2–3, Very high <2 — [Bishop et al. 2025, ACSM–ESSA](https://www.fisiologiadelejercicio.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/Physical-Activity-and-Exercise-Intensity-Terminology.pdf)
- RIR self-ratings are accurate to about 1 rep only within 0–3 RIR — [Helms et al. 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2018.00247/full). Accuracy improves near failure and with heavier loads — [Halperin et al. 2022](https://pure.solent.ac.uk/ws/portalfiles/portal/52968871/accuracy_in_predicting_repetitions_to_task_failure_in_resistance_exercise.pdf)
- Resistance exercise at ≥70% 1RM is "vigorous" — [Garber et al. 2011, ACSM position stand](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0b013e318213fefb)
- People do about 5 reps at 90% 1RM and about 15 at 70%, with SD 2.5–4.4 reps — [Nuzzo et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10933212/)
- Epley should be used with ≤10 reps; it had the smallest residuals among linear equations at 5RM — [Reynolds et al. 2006](https://www.unm.edu/~rrobergs/478RMStrengthPrediction.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Option A (least change): keep the ratio, change the threshold to ≈0.93** (RIR ≤ 3 under Epley). Cite ACSM–ESSA 2025 Table 2 for "High = 2–3 RIR" and Reynolds 2006 for Epley validity. Remaining interpretations to disclose: (1) inferring RIR by inverting Epley, and (2) using historical best e1RM as the 1RM.
- **Option B (fully load-based, older standard): mean load ÷ best e1RM ≥ 0.70 → vigorous.** This is Garber 2011 Table 5 verbatim, with "vigorous" as the exact word. Remaining interpretation: substituting e1RM for 1RM. Weakness: ACSM–ESSA 2025 now discourages %1RM. It also cannot detect bodybuilding-style sets taken to failure at lighter loads, which was the student's original reason for the ratio.
- **Option C (most rigorous): estimate RIR with the Nuzzo 2024 table** (predicted max reps at load ÷ best e1RM, minus reps done), then classify with ACSM–ESSA RIR ≤ 3. Every step is cited. It still inherits the historical-best-e1RM assumption and Nuzzo's large between-person SD.
- **No-history fallback (reps ≤ 7).** Under Nuzzo's main model, 7 reps to failure corresponds to roughly 85% 1RM; this uses the unverified secondary value and must be confirmed against Fig. 2. That meets Garber's "near maximal"/vigorous band only if the set was taken to failure. The app cannot know that, so the fallback is also an interpretation. It could instead be justified as "load that would permit ≤7 reps".
- Under every option, mapping the class to Compendium 02050 (6.0 MET) versus 02054 (3.5 MET) remains a design choice. No literature links effort classes to MET values, and ACSM–ESSA explicitly rejects METs as intensity markers.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed validation exists for any e1RM-ratio classifier against measured energy expenditure.
- Verbatim LeSuer 1997 abstract, Steele 2017/2022 definitions, Nuzzo Fig. 2 numbers, Richens & Cleather 2014, DiStasio 2014, and the Tuchscherer chart are all unverified or not found.
