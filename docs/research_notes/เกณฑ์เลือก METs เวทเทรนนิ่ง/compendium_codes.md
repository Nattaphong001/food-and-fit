## 1. Exact wording and MET values of each resistance / calisthenics / circuit code, 2011 vs 2024, and which ones changed

### Takeaway
The three codes the app uses exist exactly as the thesis states in 2024: 02050 = 6.0, 02054 = 3.5, 02056 = 3.0. However, the Compendium's own wording does not label them "heavy/moderate/light". 02050 is "power lifting or body building, vigorous effort (Taylor Code 210)". 02054 is "multiple exercises, 8-15 reps at varied resistance", with no intensity word. 02056 is body-weight exercises, "general", and its high-intensity counterpart is 02057 (6.5). 02050 and 02054 kept the same values from 2011 to 2024. 02055, 02056 and 02057 were added in 2024, and 02020, 02035 and 02040 changed value.

### Inferences
- The thesis maps 02056 to "light". The Compendium itself calls 02056 "general" and pairs it with 02057 "high intensity" (6.5). The only codes the Compendium literally labels "light effort" are the calisthenics code 02024 (2.8) and circuit training 02034 (3.5). The chapter should say "general body-weight resistance exercise", not "light".
- 02054 has no intensity adjective at all. Calling it "moderate" is valid only in the Compendium's MET-band sense (3.0–5.9 METs = moderate, see section 2), not because the description says so.
- 02050, 02054, 02052 and 02022 kept identical values from 2011 to 2024. A thesis that cites both Ainsworth et al. (2011) and Herrmann et al. (2024) for these codes is therefore internally consistent. 02020 (8.0 → 7.5) and 02035 (4.3 → 5.0) are not.

### Gaps
- I could not open the Ainsworth 2011 MSSE article or its official supplement (403 or CAPTCHA). The 2011 wording is taken from the official 2011 tracking guide, website and reference list, which should be equivalent but were not checked against the journal PDF.

## 2. Operational definitions of "light", "moderate" and "vigorous effort" in resistance training, and whether "8-15 repetitions" is a criterion for moderate

### Takeaway
The Compendium gives no operational criteria (%1RM, reps to failure, RIR, RPE, rest interval) for "light/moderate/vigorous effort" in resistance training. Its effort words are "qualitative information … general or perceived intensity level". The only numeric resistance descriptor anywhere in these codes is "8-15 reps at varied resistance", and it belongs to 02054 as a description of the activity that was measured. It is not stated as a rule that 8–15 reps means moderate. Separately, the Compendium defines intensity classes by the MET value itself: light 1.6–2.9, moderate 3.0–5.9, vigorous ≥6.0.

### Cited Findings
- "The activity descriptions provide quantitative details (e.g., bicycling pedal cadence, riding speed, and grade) and qualitative information (e.g., self-selected pace, general or perceived intensity level as light, moderate, and vigorous effort)." — [Herrmann2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10818145/)
- "The Compendium classifies sedentary behaviors as 1.0–1.5 METs, light-intensity PAs as 1.6–2.9 METs, moderate-intensity PAs as 3.0–5.9 METs, and vigorous-intensity PAs as ≥6.0 METs." — [Herrmann2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10818145/)
- The pacompendium Corrected METs page cites the U.S. Physical Activity Guidelines definitions: "light < 3.0 METs, moderate 3.0-5.9 METs, and vigorous ≥ 6.0 METs." — [CorrMET](https://pacompendium.com/corrected-mets/)
- "The associated activity description provides additional context to help identify the most appropriate intensity level." In the paper this is illustrated with video-game codes, not resistance training. — [Herrmann2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10818145/)
- The only reps/load descriptors in the resistance codes are:
  - 02054 "8-15 reps at varied resistance"
  - 02052 "slow or explosive effort"
  - 02050 "power lifting or body building, vigorous effort"
  - 02057 "high intensity" and 02056 "general"

  None of the 2024 or 2011 descriptions mentions %1RM, failure, or rest time. The only rest wording is 02040 "minimal rest" (circuit). — [C2024-web](https://pacompendium.com/conditioning-exercise/); [T2011](https://www.udg.edu/ca/portals/57/OContent_Docs/2011_Compendium_Tracking_Guide_1_.pdf)
- The studies averaged into 02054 (3.5) describe the protocols as "1 set of 8 reps", "3 sets of 8 reps", "1 set of 15 reps" (mean of 8 exercises), and "2 sets of 8 reps, young adults, mean of upper and lower body lifts, **85% maximal load**". So the "8-15" wording comes from the 8-rep and 15-rep protocols that were measured. — [R2011-cond](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1Otyetus94Ti1kVLfdNvKYrCW6Veoekzw/view)
- The measured sub-activities under 02052 (5.0) are defined by %1RM: "squat explosive effort (60% 1 RM)" 5.05; "squat slow effort (60% 1 RM)" 4.46; "squat explosive effort (80% 1 RM)" 4.34; "squat (70% 1 RM)" 5.77. — [R2011-cond](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1Otyetus94Ti1kVLfdNvKYrCW6Veoekzw/view)

### Inferences
- The thesis rule (from `CLAUDE.md` 7[B-1]) says "Reps ≤ 7 → heavy/6.0 because the moderate code is 8–15 reps". This is a reading of 02054's description, not a criterion the Compendium states. The Compendium never says that fewer than 8 reps qualifies for 02050. If kept, it must be presented as the author's interpretation.
- The Compendium's own data cut against a load-based escalation rule:
  - 02054 includes Morgan et al. (2003) at **85% of maximal load**, which gave only 2.1 METs (men) and 2.6 METs (women).
  - Under 02052, the heavier 80% 1RM squat (4.34) cost less than the 60% 1RM explosive squat (5.05).

  In the measured studies, heavier %1RM did not give a higher MET. Aerobic energy cost in resistance exercise tracks work density (reps × time under tension, short rest) more than relative load. A committee member could use this against an "Effort Ratio ≥0.90 → 6.0 METs" rule.
- Since "vigorous" in the Compendium's scheme simply means ≥6.0 METs, saying "02050 is vigorous" is circular. It restates the MET value and does not define what training qualifies.

### Gaps
- I found no Compendium document, in 1993, 2000, 2011 or 2024, that gives numeric thresholds for "vigorous effort" in weight lifting (e.g., %1RM, RPE, sets to failure). I searched the 2024 paper, both official websites and the 2011 per-code reference list. External sources such as ACSM or NSCA would be needed for such thresholds; they are outside this note's scope.

## 3. The source studies behind 02050 (6.0), 02054 (3.5), 02056 (3.0) and related codes

### Takeaway
The official 2011 per-code reference list shows the following:
- **02054 (3.5)** is the "Average of 8 measures" from four indirect-calorimetry studies (Haddock & Wilkin 2006; Phillips & Ziuraitis 2003, 2004; Morgan et al. 2003).
- **02050 (6.0)** has only one reference, Taylor, Jacobs et al. (1978), a leisure-time physical activity **questionnaire** paper whose "Taylor Code 210" the Compendium inherited. There is no calorimetry study for 02050.
- **02052 (5.0)** averages five squat measures.
- **02056 (3.0)** and **02057 (6.5)** are new in 2024. I could not locate their source studies.

### Inferences
- **02050 = 6.0 has the weakest evidence of the three codes.** In the official 2011 reference list its only source is the 1978 Minnesota leisure-time questionnaire paper (Taylor Code 210). No indirect-calorimetry measurement of powerlifting or bodybuilding is listed. It is still coloured "supported" (blue in 2011, not red in 2024), presumably because Taylor et al. is a published source. The thesis can cite 02050 honestly, but should not claim that 6.0 was measured on heavy lifting.
- 02054 = 3.5 is well grounded in measured whole-session resistance training, both single-set and multi-set, with 8–15 reps, including a heavy (85% max load) protocol. The Compendium's own evidence suggests that most ordinary gym sessions, including heavy ones, measure around 2–4 METs.
- 02056 (3.0) and 02057 (6.5) are marked supported (not red) in 2024, but I could not verify their underlying studies.

### Gaps
- The source studies for 2024-only codes 02055 (5.8), 02056 (3.0), 02057 (6.5), 02032, 02034 and 02058, and for the 2024 value changes to 02020 (7.5), 02035 (5.0) and 02040 (7.5), are unverified. They are probably held in the 2024 Compendium's internal data-extraction files, which I could not find online. Possible next steps: email the Compendium team via pacompendium.com, or look for a 2024 reference list by major heading if one is published later.
- I did not open the individual source studies (Haddock 2006, Phillips 2003/2004, Morgan 2003, Mazzetti 2007, Bloomer 2005) to confirm the numbers. Figures above are as reported in the Compendium's reference list.

## 4. What the Compendium says about selecting a code, its intended uses, and its limitations

### Takeaway
The Compendium was built to give consistent MET values for coding questionnaires in epidemiology. Its authors say it does not give precise energy expenditure for individuals: it "can serve as a starting point". For code selection, the only guidance is to match the activity description ("The associated activity description provides additional context to help identify the most appropriate intensity level"). There are no decision rules for converting training variables (load, reps, rest) into a code. For individual estimates, the website suggests corrected METs (Harris-Benedict RMR) as an optional adjustment.

### Cited Findings
- "The Compendium of Physical Activities (Compendium) was developed to address consistent assignment of physical activity (PA) intensity values used in PA epidemiology research." — [History2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10818106/)
- "The Compendium provides a consistent MET value, based whenever possible on published studies of energy cost, for scoring PA questionnaires, allowing for improved comparability across studies." — [Herrmann2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10818145/)
- "Though the original purpose of the Compendium was to support epidemiological research in the assignment of MET values and to improve comparability across studies, many users have attempted to use the Compendium to calculate individual EE estimates and develop exercise prescriptions in clinical settings. Several factors can influence RMR and the EE of PA; thus, the Compendium can serve as a starting point for prescribing individual activities but does not reflect precise individual EE values." — [Herrmann2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10818145/)
- "The Compendium was not developed to determine the precise energy cost of activities within an individual. However, the Compendium is frequently used outside of its original scope." — [CorrMET](https://pacompendium.com/corrected-mets/)
- "It does not correct the MET levels for age, body mass, and sex." — [CorrMET](https://pacompendium.com/corrected-mets/)
- "the corrected MET level may be appropriate for use by clinicians and fitness professionals who use the Compendium to develop individualized exercise prescriptions and to estimate individual daily physical activity energy expenditure. The standard MET levels published in the Compendium should be used to classify the intensity of physical activities in survey research settings…" — [CorrMET](https://pacompendium.com/corrected-mets/)
- "neither the standard nor the corrected MET values replace the direct assessment of RMR or energy expenditure and do not remove all error and misclassification identified from direct measurement." — [CorrMET](https://pacompendium.com/corrected-mets/)
- The standard MET is defined as "1 MET = 3.5 mL/kg/min". "Kozey et al. have proposed a corrected MET to adjust for individual differences in RMR using the Harris-Benedict RMR equation." — [Herrmann2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10818145/)
- Kozey et al. found that the standard MET "misclassifies the intensity category 12.2% of the time compared to measured MET values with greater misclassification in individuals that are overweight, older, low fit, or women." — [CorrMET](https://pacompendium.com/corrected-mets/)
- Dose computation: "MET-minutes and MET-hours … are computed by multiplying the Specific Activity MET value by the duration of the reported PA in minutes or hours." — [Herrmann2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10818145/)
- Code structure: 5 digits, "Major Headings (first 2 digits) and Specific Activity codes (last 3 digits), followed by their associated standard MET value and activity description." — [Herrmann2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10818145/)
- On choosing a code: "The associated activity description provides additional context to help identify the most appropriate intensity level." — [Herrmann2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10818145/)
- Population scope: "intended for use in non-disabled adults and adults without significant health conditions that affect EE or metabolism"; the adult list is limited to ages 19–59, with a separate Older Adult Compendium for ≥60. — [Herrmann2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10818145/)

### Inferences
- There is no official Compendium algorithm for mapping logged set data (load, reps, %1RM, rest) to a resistance code. Any such rule, including the app's "Effort Ratio ≥ 0.90 → 02050" and "no history + reps ≤ 7 → 02050", is necessarily the developer's interpretation.
- The committee's request for "published backing" can be met only partly:
  - The Compendium backs the MET values and the descriptions.
  - The selection thresholds need other literature (e.g., ACSM/NSCA intensity zones), or should be stated openly as the author's interpretation.
  - The Compendium itself advises matching the activity description.
- The app's kcal formula uses net METs, (MET − 1), with the standard 3.5 mL/kg/min. That approach is not Compendium text, which describes gross MET × time. It is also not the Compendium's "corrected MET". It needs its own citation (e.g., ACSM metabolic equations), which is outside this note's scope.

### Gaps
- I could not check the 2011 MSSE paper for its own usage/limitation wording (paywall/CAPTCHA). The 2000 paper (Ainsworth et al., MSSE 32(9 Suppl):S498–S504, doi:10.1097/00005768-200009001-00009) was also not retrieved. The 1993/2000 papers are commonly said to include "individual differences" caveats, but I have not verified the verbatim text.

## 5. Do Ainsworth, Herrmann or the Compendium website say anything about apps and wearables?

### Takeaway
Yes, but only descriptively. The authors acknowledge that the Compendium is embedded in gym equipment, apps and mobile technologies, and say that the 2024 version has utility "in the development of consumer health technologies". They give no app-specific validation or guidance. Energy estimates from wearables or prediction equations were excluded as source data.

### Cited Findings
- "The Compendium also has been embedded in commercial products developed to determine the energy expended on gym equipment and in apps to record and track PA and its energy expenditure." — [History2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10818106/)
- "It has also appeared in mobile technologies designed for exercise and weight management, and has been applied in educational settings as well as public health and health promotion settings." — [Herrmann2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10818145/)
- "…The 2024 Adult Compendium, which has utility across research, public health, education, and healthcare domains, as well as in the development of consumer health technologies." — [Herrmann2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10818145/)
- "The Compendium covers energy costs of individual PAs across PA and health studies, with updates extending to other areas, including personal health and fitness programming and individual PA assessment settings." — [Herrmann2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10818145/)
- Source data exclusion: studies were excluded if "the estimated EE of PAs was from heart rate monitors, motion sensors, or prediction equations (e.g., Polar, SenseWear, ActiGraph accelerometer, or American College of Sports Medicine prediction equations)." — [Herrmann2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10818145/)
- pacompendium.com offers a "Compendium Calculator" and a "Corrected METs" calculator: https://pacompendium.com/compendium-calculator/ and https://pacompendium.com/corrected-mets/. I only confirmed the Corrected METs page content. — [CorrMET](https://pacompendium.com/corrected-mets/)

### Inferences
- A thesis can legitimately cite Herrmann et al. (2024) and Ainsworth et al. (2024, History) to justify using Compendium METs in a consumer app. It should pair that with the authors' own caveat that values are "a starting point" and "not precise individual EE values".

### Gaps
- I found no statement from Ainsworth or Herrmann specifically about how an app should choose among resistance-training codes, or validating Compendium-based resistance-training kcal against measured values in app users.
